Investing in US stocks from India: my notes on choosing a broker

Sony Mathew
November 15, 2026 • 6 min read

I’ve been researching platforms for investing in US stocks from India. My shortlist included Interactive Brokers, INDmoney, Vested, and the global investing services offered by Indian brokers such as ICICI Direct, HDFC Securities, and Motilal Oswal.

I wanted to understand how much each option would cost and how much work I would need to do myself. Buying a stock is only one part of the process. There’s also transferring money, converting rupees to dollars, maintaining records, filing taxes, and eventually bringing the money back.

Once I started looking at those steps together, the comparison became more useful.

These are my notes from that research. The pricing references below were checked on 15 November 2026.

The first thing I’d compare is how much money actually reaches the investment account.

Brokerage is easy to find on a pricing page. Currency conversion takes a little more attention. A platform might advertise free deposits, but the exchange rate still affects how many dollars you receive.

For example, a hypothetical 1% currency conversion cost on ₹5 lakh works out to ₹5,000. A ₹1,000 transfer fee on the same amount is 0.2%. But that transfer fee could sit alongside a currency markup, so comparing those two numbers alone would be misleading.

I’d ask each provider for a quote using the same transfer amount, at roughly the same time: how many dollars will reach my account after all charges? I’d also check the cost of converting the money back.

This matters when evaluating IBKR. If my Indian bank converts the rupees before sending dollars, that bank’s exchange rate still applies. Opening an IBKR account does not, by itself, remove the currency conversion cost.

The brokerage comparison also needs more care than a “free versus paid” label suggests.

For someone making a few purchases a year, the result can look quite different from someone buying several stocks every month. I’d calculate the cost using my expected transactions before deciding which pricing model is cheaper.

The next consideration is the amount of administration I’m comfortable with.

For regular investing, I can see the appeal of INDmoney and Vested. Funding support, recurring purchases, and Indian tax documents are useful parts of the product. Vested, for example, provides reports covering foreign assets and income, including information for Schedule FA. That would carry real weight in my comparison. Vested’s tax reporting update

I’d still want to see a sample report and check it with my CA. A downloadable statement is useful only if it contains the information needed for my situation.

For IBKR, I would pay closer attention to the funding process and how its statements would fit into my tax preparation. Any savings need to justify the extra work involved.

I would also compare the specific investments I want to buy. Broad platform labels can hide meaningful differences. INDmoney now lists global UCITS access, and Vested offers global funds alongside its US investing products. The relevant question is whether the particular fund, exchange, or security I need is available through my account. INDmoney’s product coverage, Vested’s offerings

Traditional Indian brokers deserve the same individual comparison. My initial grouping was too broad. ICICI Direct, for instance, lists access to six global markets and offers a plan without an annual subscription. I’d evaluate the actual service and quote before drawing conclusions about the whole category. ICICI Direct Global

There are also a few rules that affect the decision regardless of the platform.

For overseas investment remittances under LRS, the standard TCS rate is currently 20% on the amount above the ₹10 lakh annual threshold. TCS is creditable against tax liability, but it still affects how much cash needs to be available when transferring money. That makes it relevant to planning the investment amount. ICICI Bank’s current remittance guidance

For an Indian resident individual eligible for treaty benefits, ordinary US dividends generally face 25% US withholding. Submitting Form W-8BEN helps establish eligibility for the applicable treaty treatment; it does not generally reduce that rate to 15%. India–US tax treaty, Article 10, IRS treaty guidance

I also wouldn’t count every feature advertised by a global broker as available to an Indian resident. RBI’s LRS rules prohibit remittances for margins or margin calls to overseas exchanges or counterparties. Account eligibility and local restrictions need to be checked before treating advanced trading features as a benefit. RBI’s LRS directions

After going through this, I’m less convinced by a fixed rule such as “use a fintech below ₹5 lakh and switch to IBKR above ₹15 lakh.”

The amount matters, but so do transfer frequency, order size, the bank’s exchange rate, and the investments I want to hold.

For smaller, regular investments, I would give considerable weight to convenient funding and useful tax reports. For larger transfers or access to particular overseas markets, I would compare IBKR closely against the other options using actual quotes. An existing banking relationship could also make a traditional broker worth considering.

For my own investing, I would choose IBKR.

The main reason is flexibility. I want access to markets beyond the US, with room to explore other stocks, ETFs, and bonds as my investing needs change. IBKR’s overseas account for Indian residents gives me that broader access.

I’d also be comfortable starting with smaller amounts once I’ve set up the first wire transfer and understood the funding process. There’s some work upfront, but I’d expect subsequent transfers to be more familiar. I’d still pay attention to fixed transfer charges and how often I send money, since those costs matter more on small remittances.

What appeals to me is having more control over each step: choosing the bank for the transfer, comparing its currency conversion quote, deciding when to fund the account, and placing my own orders. I’m comfortable doing that setup if it gives me more flexibility over time.

The main caveat for me is tax filing. I couldn’t find ready-made Indian tax reports in IBKR’s published reporting options. It provides account statements and relevant tax forms, but I’d need to work through those records myself or with my CA to prepare the Indian tax return.

That’s a trade-off I’m willing to make. For me, the broader market access and control are worth the extra work at tax time.

Zerodha has already started rolling out US stock and ETF investing in beta, and is exploring UCITS ETFs and access to other markets. I’ll be watching how that develops, especially around broader market access, funding, and Indian tax reporting. IBKR would still be my choice today, but this is a comparison I’d revisit as those offerings mature.
